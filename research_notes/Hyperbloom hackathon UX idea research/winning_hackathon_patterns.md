## 1. Recent (2023–2026) winning projects from UI/UX and design hackathons

### Takeaway
The verifiable recent winners fall into four groups. (a) Research-backed student-life or community apps at collegiate design-a-thons (Rice, UCR, MSU, FigBuild). (b) Redesigns of real products that fix a named pain point (Bank of America, Goodreads, MSU dining, Moldova's MPay). (c) Novel interaction concepts at Figma's Make-a-thon, such as an async photo booth, a collaborative embroidery sampler and a 16-bit explorable Tokyo. (d) Emerging-tech concepts with evidence of user research at the World Usability Day challenge. Across the groups, winners pair one specific user and problem with a clickable, polished prototype and a rehearsed pitch.

### Inferences
- Student design-a-thon winners rarely pursue novelty for its own sake. They win on a narrow, felt problem (finding a roommate, concussion recovery, dining-hall dietary needs, a cluttered banking app) plus visible process evidence (surveys, secondary research) plus craft (a design system and a polished prototype).
- Open, non-student contests such as Figma's Make-a-thon reward interaction novelty and craft over research rigor. Their categories are Excellence in Craft, New Interaction and Reimagining Iconic Interactions. A team should read which type of event it is entering.
- "Redesign" is a winnable category in its own right (ACM-W x UXD, MSU Best Redesign, UX4Gov MPay). A redesign that cites concrete pain points in a real product is a lower-risk path for a small team.
- The ACM-W winners update has ID 35042, close to the WLH demo-tips update 36832 from mid-2025. This suggests the ACM-W event was around early-to-mid 2025, but that is not confirmed.

## 2. What judges explicitly say they look for (rubrics and judge quotes)

### Takeaway
Design-a-thon rubrics converge on four or five criteria:
- user research grounded in evidence
- design thinking: a clear rationale linking insight to decision
- UX/usability: intuitive, easy to navigate
- visual design/craft: hierarchy, typography, color, polish
- presentation/storytelling

Accessibility is increasingly scored or given its own prize. Judges also say that missing basic requirements, ambiguity, and polish without substance lose points.

### Cited Findings
- **Rice Design-a-thon 2025 rubric** has four criteria. **User Experience** and **Visual Design** are listed without further detail. **User Research** asks the team to collect "data from a variety of credible sources relevant to the user group, conducted through both general and specific lenses." **Design Thinking** asks for "clearly provided reasoning for design decisions." — [Rice 2025 Devpost](https://rice-design-a-thon-2025.devpost.com/). The 2024 Nest case study lists the same four areas, with visual design scored out of 12. — [Mandy Lai](https://mandyslai.com/rice-designathon)
- **Technica 2024 "Best UI/UX Hack"** is judged on innovation, aesthetics, service to the project or challenge, and *process*. Technica also runs a separate **Best Accessibility Solution** prize. Technica 2025's general criteria include whether a project considers "accessibility, representation, user safety, empowerment." — [Technica 2024 Devpost](https://technica-2024.devpost.com/); [Technica 2025 Devpost](https://technica-2025.devpost.com/)
- **Hack The Map 2024 (UVA) Best UI/UX Hack** factors in "aesthetics, innovation, usability, and service to the project." — [Hack The Map 2024 Devpost](https://hackthemap2024.devpost.com/)
- **HackTX 2025 Best Design ($1,000):** "The end user experience is one of the most important parts of an app. This prize seeks clean assets, ease-of-use, and sensibly designed projects." — [HackTX 2025 Devpost](https://hacktx2025.devpost.com/)
- **HackUSF 2025** scores design 1–10 each on visual quality, user experience, interactivity and attention to detail. One judge question is "Is it intuitive and easy to navigate?" — [HackUSF Devpost](https://hackusf.devpost.com/)
- **nwHacks 2025 Best Design** looks for creativity, functionality and UX. A separate community-impact prize rewards "designing with empathy and building for accessibility." — [nwHacks 2025 Devpost](https://nwhacks-2025.devpost.com/). **Hack for Humanity (Summer 2026)** has a "UI/UX & Accessibility" judging category. — [Hack for Humanity Devpost](https://hack-for-humanity-summer-26.devpost.com/)
- **FigBuild 2026** uses two-stage judging. Stage One is a pass/fail check on viability; Stage Two uses equally weighted criteria. — [FigBuild 2026 rules](https://figbuild2026.devpost.com/rules). A third-party guide lists the Figma contests' criteria as design quality and craft, innovation and creativity, storytelling and presentation, Figma Make integration, and a People's Choice vote. This is a secondary source and may not match the official rules. — [thenewviews](https://thenewviews.com/figma-makeathon/)
  - The sources disagree on a storytelling prize. One search summary listed "Best Design" and "Best Storytelling" prizes for FigBuild 2026; another found only First Place, Best Design and Most Creative.
- **MSU Designathon rubric** evaluates "the research supporting the problem": a well-scoped problem "rests on solid evidence, data, or case studies," and testing should validate the solution. Its tracks and awards:
  - Tracks: Accessibility & Inclusivity, Sustainability (UN SDGs), Education & Learning.
  - Awards: Best DEI, Best Prototype, Best Redesign, Organizer's Favorite.
  - Source: [MSU Designathon Devpost](https://msu-designathon.devpost.com/)
- **UCSD Pepper Canyon Designathon:** teams must "show how this problem was determined through evidence of existing conditions, such as interviews with users, images, videos, or plans." — [Pepper Canyon Designathon](https://sites.google.com/ucsd.edu/peppercanyondesignathon/home)
- **UCR DesignVerse** asked for products "focused on creativity, accessibility and authenticity." Its visual-design track judged visual weight, hierarchy, scale, typography and color palette. — [DesignVerse 2024 Devpost](https://designverseucr.devpost.com/); [UCR blog](https://business.ucr.edu/blog/2024/06/16/ucr-students-organize-first-uiux-design-thon)
- **World Usability Day Design Challenge** (2024 call) required "evidence of user research and evaluation that had a clear and valuable impact on interaction, product and/or service design recommendations and decisions." The 2025 call asked for fidelity "sufficiently complete to allow meaningful user testing." — [WUD 2024 page](https://2025.hci.international/World_Usability_Day.html); [WUD 2025 page](https://2026.hci.international/World_Usability_Day.html)
- **UX Design Awards jury:** solutions must rest on "real insights from dedicated user research." — [UX Design Awards judging criteria](https://ux-design-awards.com/jury/judging-criteria)
- **Devpost's general guide:**
  - Typical criteria are "technological implementation, ease of use, demonstration, potential impact, quality of the idea, and design."
  - "A clean, functional, and professional design can go a long way."
  - "If you can't explain how to use your project in a sentence or two, it might be too complicated."
  - Tailor your submission to each event's rubric.
  - Source: [Devpost: understanding judging criteria](https://info.devpost.com/blog/understanding-hackathon-submission-and-judging-criteria)
- **Devpost "5 seasoned judges":**
  - Karen checks the hackathon's requirements first, noting that many entries miss basic requirements, then looks for creativity and enthusiasm.
  - Kelvin (Flutter): "the first thing we look at is how visually appealing the project is," then whether the demo performs as intended.
  - Richard (Square): judges evaluate each project against the criteria individually rather than comparing entries.
  - "Ambiguity is a red flag, and also projects that lack detail."
  - Sources: [Devpost blog](https://info.devpost.com/blog/hackathon-judging-tips); [Devpost LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/devpost_how-to-win-a-hackathon-advice-from-5-seasoned-activity-7138211060154634240-EjbW)
- **Organizer guidance on design rubrics:**
  - Captivate defines **UX** as "intuitive design, ease of use, and overall user journey," **Visual Design** as "aesthetic appeal, branding consistency, and visual polish," and **Accessibility** as usability for diverse abilities, ideally scored by running standardized accessibility checks. It also suggests weighting subjective visual scores lower, or giving them a separate prize. — [Captivate](https://captivate.dev/making-your-hackathon-a-success-setup/)
  - HackathonHost recommends 4–6 criteria, with an example weighting of Design & UX at 20%. — [HackathonHost](https://hackathonhost.com/articles/hackathon-judging-criteria)
- **Other judge perspectives:**
  - A Mercer judge: "A well-defined problem statement often reveals more maturity than the code itself." — [Mercer/Mettl](https://blog.mettl.com/hackathon-judging-criteria/)
  - Another judge asks: "Do they see the problem behind the solution, or are they just trying to fit their solution to this particular hackathon?" — [Mercer/Mettl](https://blog.mettl.com/hackathon-judging-criteria/)
  - JetBrains judges (2026): a simpler project with a clear, confident demo beats a complex one with a confusing demo. — [JetBrains blog](https://blog.jetbrains.com/ai/2026/06/how-to-win-a-hackathon-notes-from-the-judging-table/)

### Inferences
- For a design-a-thon, the safest reading of the rubric is an even split across research, rationale, usability, visual craft and presentation. Visual polish is usually only about a quarter of the score: Rice scores visual design out of 12, and Captivate advises weighting visuals lower. Pretty screens alone cannot win.
- "Process" and "design thinking" are scored explicitly (Technica, Rice). The write-up therefore has to make the line from insight to decision visible, for example "we found X in interviews, so we designed Y."
- Accessibility is moving from a nice-to-have to a scored criterion or a track (Technica, MSU, nwHacks, Hack for Humanity, Catalyst 2024's Accessibility track, UCR). A short WCAG check is a cheap differentiator.

## 3. How to structure a hackathon UX case study, and which artefacts impress

### Takeaway
NN/g's case-study model fits a design-a-thon write-up compressed to 48 hours. It runs: problem or hypothesis → your role → research and how you reached the solution → whether it worked (testing) → challenges and rejected concepts → impact. The artefacts with the best evidence of helping winners are named research (surveys with an n, secondary sources), a design system or component set, a clickable hi-fi prototype, explicit design rationale, and before/after views for redesigns.

### Cited Findings
- **NN/g's six components** for a case study: the problem or hypothesis; your role and collaboration; how you reached the solution; whether it worked; challenges, including rejected concepts; and the effect on users and the business. NN/g also advises:
  - Include early sketches, whiteboards and research documentation, because "final screenshots only tell part of the story."
  - Use a consistent template: title page, intro, captioned images, results.
  - Source: [NN/g: 5 Steps to Creating a UX-Design Portfolio](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)
- NN/g's case-study video frames the structure as describing the design, the UX process that led to it, and its business impact. — [NN/g video](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/ux-design-portfolio-case-study/)
- The **Nest** (2nd, Rice 2024) deliverables were secondary research, user surveys, an interactive prototype and a presentation. Its standout artefact was a team design system built mid-hackathon, and it scored 11/12 on visual design. — [Mandy Lai](https://mandyslai.com/rice-designathon)
- The Rice rubric explicitly rewards research from "a variety of credible sources... general and specific lenses" and "clearly provided reasoning for design decisions." — [Rice 2025 Devpost](https://rice-design-a-thon-2025.devpost.com/)
- Prototype fidelity is sometimes a hard requirement. UCR's prototyping track required "high-fidelity wireframes and interactive, functional, animated designs." — [UCR blog](https://business.ucr.edu/blog/2024/06/16/ucr-students-organize-first-uiux-design-thon). WUD required fidelity "sufficiently complete to allow meaningful user testing." — [WUD 2025](https://2026.hci.international/World_Usability_Day.html)
- **Redesign winners** led with named pain points. Bank of America's entry cited a "cluttered interface and frustrating chatbot"; the Goodreads entry focused on discovery and community. — [ACM-W x UXD winners](https://acm-w-uxd-figma-hackathon.devpost.com/updates/35042-figma-hackathon-winners)
- Typical 48-hour designathon case studies (not confirmed winners) use **heuristic evaluation, then a Figma interactive prototype, then a retest with users**. Examples: a travel app team that presented to 120 participants, 7 alumni and 1 mentor, and the AJIO team's single search-to-filter flow for working women. — [48 Hours Revamping UX Journey](https://medium.com/@punnaanilkumar96/48-hours-revamping-ux-journey-a-designathon-case-study-6dfc8b11a05a); [AJIO designathon case study](https://medium.com/uxm-community/ux-case-study-a-designathon-case-study-on-ajio-6a2ac0bfeeba)
- **Using SUS honestly:** NN/g says questionnaires like SUS need "at least 20–30 users" to generalize, and "numeric data from 5 users should not inform design decisions." The average SUS across 500 studies is 68 (Sauro). — [NN/g: Beyond NPS — SUS, NASA-TLX, SEQ](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/measuring-perceived-usability/)
- **Accessibility audit anchors (WCAG 2.2 AA):**
  - Text contrast at least 4.5:1, or 3:1 for large text. Thresholds are not rounded, so 4.499:1 fails.
  - Pointer targets at least 24×24 CSS px, unless spacing exceptions apply.
  - Sources: [W3C Understanding 1.4.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html); [W3C Understanding 2.5.8](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum.html); [WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)

### Inferences
- The artefacts most likely to impress, ranked by what rubrics score:
  1. A research summary with numbers: n surveyed, n interviewed, 2–3 quoted insights.
  2. A how-might-we statement plus one persona tied to the insights.
  3. One core user flow or journey map, not a full information architecture of the whole app.
  4. Design rationale callouts on the hi-fi screens ("because users said X").
  5. A mini design system: color, type and 6–10 components.
  6. Test results from about 5 users, given as task success and the top issues fixed, shown as before→after iterations.
  7. A WCAG contrast and target-size check.
- Report SUS only as indicative with n=5, or skip it. Claiming a "SUS of 85" from five friends goes against NN/g guidance, and a knowledgeable judge may discount it. Task-completion rates and observed issues are more defensible.
- For redesign prompts, a side-by-side before/after with annotated pain points is the expected core artefact.

## 4. Demo video best practices

### Takeaway
Keep it under 3 minutes and script it. State the user and problem in the first few seconds. Spend more time on the why than on a feature tour (one 22-time winner uses a 60/40 explain-to-demo split). Walk one realistic scenario through the clickable prototype, and upload early.

### Cited Findings
- Devpost's "6 Tips for making a winning hackathon demo video": plan early; prepare a script; "most hackathons require demo videos to be less than three minutes." Tips 3–6 were not retrievable. — [Devpost blog](https://info.devpost.com/blog/6-tips-for-making-a-hackathon-demo-video)
- The Devpost Help Center says to explain what your app does in the first few seconds and not to leave the upload to the last minute, since processing can take "anywhere from a few minutes to several hours or more." — [Devpost Help: video-making best practices](https://help.devpost.com/article/84-video-making-best-practices)
- The World's Largest Hackathon (Bolt, 2025) demo-video update says:
  - The video is often the first thing judges watch.
  - Cover the requirements, the tools used, the problem and the solution.
  - Write and practice a script.
  - Show the project working in real-world scenarios.
  - Upload early.
  - Source: [WLH Devpost update](https://worldslargesthackathon.devpost.com/updates/36832-demo-video-tips)
- Nathan, a 22-time Devpost winner: "a good rule of thumb is to spend 60% of the time explaining and 40% actually demoing." — [Devpost user story: Nathan](https://info.devpost.com/blog/user-story-nathan)
- Other guides:
  - Aim for about 3 minutes.
  - Open with the team and project name, the track, and the challenge addressed.
  - Make the target audience clear early, show the best features and user flows, and include a try-it link.
  - YouTube videos "can be unlisted but must be viewable via the link."
  - Source: [Meta Creator forum: tips for your Devpost video](https://communityforums.atmeta.com/discussions/Creator_Discussion/tips-for-your-devpost-competition-video/1309904)
- A Databricks judge: "Storytelling within the video and the text description helps keep judges engaged and focused." — [Mercer/Mettl](https://blog.mettl.com/hackathon-judging-criteria/)
- The Nest team (2nd, Rice 2024) set a hard design cutoff so they could rehearse their pitch. — [Mandy Lai](https://mandyslai.com/rice-designathon)

### Inferences
- A suggested 2:30–3:00 storyline for a Figma-prototype submission:
  1. **0:00–0:15.** Hook: one user, one painful moment, ideally a real quote or statistic from your research.
  2. **0:15–0:45.** Research insights and the how-might-we statement.
  3. **0:45–2:15.** One continuous scenario through the clickable prototype, with voice-over naming the design decision behind each key screen. Show one before→after iteration from testing.
  4. **2:15–2:45.** Accessibility and design-system glimpse, test results, and what you would build next.
  5. **2:45–3:00.** Close with the product name and a one-line value proposition.
- Record the prototype at device resolution, show cursor or tap indicators, and add captions. Captions are accessible and also help judges watching with sound off. The captions advice is inferred, not sourced.

- I found no design-a-thon-specific video guidance from Figma, Rice or UCI organizers. Tips 3–6 of the Devpost video article were not retrievable.

## 5. Fast, credible user research in a hackathon timeframe

### Takeaway
Combine three things:
- a quick survey for breadth (winners and strong entries report n of about 40)
- secondary research or a competitor audit for credibility
- about 5 quick qualitative tests of the prototype (NN/g's discount-usability standard) for iteration evidence

Report each method with its sample size and honest limits.

### Cited Findings
- NN/g: "test 5 users in a qualitative usability study," with "little additional benefit to running more than 5 people through the same study." Nielsen's 1989 discount-usability paper advocated 3 users; he later settled on 5. — [NN/g: How many test users](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-many-test-users/); [NN/g: Discount Usability 20 Years](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/discount-usability-20-years/)
- The common "about 85% of problems found with 5 users" figure is contested. A follow-up study found that individual five-user samples found between 55% and 99% of problems, so treat 5 as a planning heuristic. — Secondary summary of the literature, via [NN/g How many test users](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-many-test-users/) and [Lyssna SUS guide](https://www.lyssna.com/blog/system-usability-scale/). *The 55–99% figure came from a search summary of secondary sources; its primary study was not retrieved.*
- SUS needs 20–30 or more users before comparing to the average of 68. — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/measuring-perceived-usability/)
- The Mango Sticky Rice team (Rice 2025) "surveyed over 40 college students from various majors and age groups using Google Forms" and drew on a secondary study on goal-setting and accountability. — [Devpost](https://devpost.com/software/rice-design-a-thon-team)
- Nest combined secondary research with user surveys. — [Mandy Lai](https://mandyslai.com/rice-designathon)
- 48-hour designathon teams used heuristic evaluation on an existing product, then retested their redesigned prototype with users. — [48 Hours Revamping UX Journey](https://medium.com/@punnaanilkumar96/48-hours-revamping-ux-journey-a-designathon-case-study-6dfc8b11a05a); [AJIO case study](https://medium.com/uxm-community/ux-case-study-a-designathon-case-study-on-ajio-6a2ac0bfeeba)
- Rubrics that reward this: Rice (credible, varied sources); MSU (evidence, data, case studies, and testing that validates the solution); Pepper Canyon (interviews, images, videos as evidence). — [Rice 2025](https://rice-design-a-thon-2025.devpost.com/); [MSU Designathon](https://msu-designathon.devpost.com/); [Pepper Canyon](https://sites.google.com/ucsd.edu/peppercanyondesignathon/home)
- Unmoderated prototype testing can be run inside Figma with plugins, for example the UXtweak Prototype Usability Testing plugin. — [Figma Community: UXtweak plugin](https://www.figma.com/community/plugin/1521469424822443095/uxtweak-prototype-usability-testing)

### Inferences
- A 48-hour research plan for a 3–4 person team:
  - **Hour 0–3:** 15–20 minutes of desk research (2–3 credible statistics or studies) and a competitor audit of 3–5 apps. Note gaps in a feature/UX matrix.
  - **Hour 1–6, in parallel:** a Google Form of 8–10 questions shared to class Discord or Slack groups, targeting 30–50 responses. Run 3–5 short interviews of 10 minutes each over video or in person, with the specific target user rather than generic friends.
  - **Hour 6–8:** synthesis with affinity clusters, 3 insights, 1 persona and a how-might-we statement.
  - **Hour 30–40:** 5 task-based tests of the clickable prototype. Use guerrilla testing in person, or unmoderated tests through a Figma plugin. Record task success and the top 3 issues, fix them, and show before→after.
  - **Optional:** heuristic evaluation against Nielsen's 10 heuristics, and a WCAG contrast check.
- Credibility comes from transparency: state each method, its n and a limitation ("5 participants, all university students"). Judges reward evidence, and an honest small sample reads better than an inflated metric.

- I found no data on whether judges actually verify research claims.

## 6. Common pitfalls that lose points

### Takeaway
Most lost points come from:
- process failures: missing requirements, a work-in-progress write-up, a late or unviewable video, or a prototype that cannot be shown to have been made during the event
- clarity failures: an ambiguous problem, a concept too complex to explain in a sentence or two
- substance failures: polish without research or rationale, or a generic idea

### Cited Findings
- Many entries miss basic requirements, and judges check these first. — [Devpost: 5 judges](https://info.devpost.com/blog/hackathon-judging-tips)
- "Ambiguity is a red flag, and also projects that lack detail." Projects that look polished but are thin tend to lose ground. — [Devpost: 5 judges](https://info.devpost.com/blog/hackathon-judging-tips)
- "If you can't explain how to use your project in a sentence or two, it might be too complicated." — [Devpost judging criteria guide](https://info.devpost.com/blog/understanding-hackathon-submission-and-judging-criteria)
- Fitting a solution to the hackathon instead of seeing the problem is a judge red flag. — [Mercer/Mettl](https://blog.mettl.com/hackathon-judging-criteria/)
- **Provenance rules:** DivHacks allows a Figma prototype or slide deck for Best Design or Best Pitch, but "version history must still show it was created during the event." — [DivHacks Devpost](https://divhacks.devpost.com/). FigBuild requires Figma EDU verification. — [Devpost on X](https://x.com/devpost/status/2028485640292360664)
- **Video logistics:** processing can take hours; some hackathons are strict and give no extensions; unlisted YouTube videos must still be viewable via the link. — [Devpost Help](https://help.devpost.com/article/84-video-making-best-practices); [Devpost video tips](https://info.devpost.com/blog/6-tips-for-making-a-hackathon-demo-video); [Meta forum](https://communityforums.atmeta.com/discussions/Creator_Discussion/tips-for-your-devpost-competition-video/1309904)
- **Unfinished write-ups:** the Rice 2025 "Mango Sticky Rice" Devpost page still had placeholder sections despite solid research. — [Devpost](https://devpost.com/software/rice-design-a-thon-team)
- **Time and scope:** Angela Han planned to finish all planning and design on day 1 but completed only two screens. — [Angela Han recap](https://medium.com/@angelahan_uiux/a-recap-of-the-rice-design-a-thon-2024-experience-7a4cf82f227f). Nest protected pitch-rehearsal time with a hard design cutoff. — [Mandy Lai](https://mandyslai.com/rice-designathon)
- **Generic ideas:** "AI chatbot for X" (generic, everyone does this), "Another todo/note app" (zero innovation) and "Dashboard that shows data" (not actionable). *These come from SEO-style blogs and are low-reliability opinion.* — [Reskilll](https://blogs.reskilll.com/hackathon-ideas-that-won-2026-judges-actually-pick-examples/); [WhereUElevate](https://whereuelevate.com/blogs/15-crazy-hackathon-ideas-that-actually-win)
- **Misusing metrics:** SUS from 5 users should not drive decisions. — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/measuring-perceived-usability/)
- **Polish alone does not win the main prize:** organizers are advised to weight subjective visual scores lower or give them a separate prize. — [Captivate](https://captivate.dev/making-your-hackathon-a-success-setup/). Rice's Angela Han won "Best UI" recognition but did not place in the top three. — [Angela Han recap](https://medium.com/@angelahan_uiux/a-recap-of-the-rice-design-a-thon-2024-experience-7a4cf82f227f)

### Inferences
- The "Dribbble-style, pretty but unusable" failure mode is implied by rubrics that score research, rationale and usability separately from visuals (Rice, Technica, HackUSF). It is also implied by Angela Han's case: best UI, but no top-three placement.
- **Broken prototype links:** Figma prototypes shared without "anyone with the link can view" permission, or links pointing at the editor rather than the presentation view, are a likely silent killer. I found no source documenting this specifically. A pre-submission checklist item is to test every link in an incognito window.
- Over-long write-ups: no direct source. Devpost's "explain it in a sentence or two" guidance points toward a scannable write-up with a one-line value proposition at the top.

### Gaps
- I found no judge quote or rubric that explicitly penalizes broken Figma permissions or overlong write-ups.
- I found no reliable source listing over-saturated ideas specifically for design-a-thons. The SEO lists are generic and low quality.

## 7. Over-saturated vs fresh idea categories in student UI/UX hackathons

### Takeaway
The student design-a-thon entries surfaced in this research cluster heavily around campus life and productivity: roommate finding, chore splitting, habit building, procrastination and accountability, dining. They also cluster around sustainability (food waste, composting) and education games. These can still place (Nest took 2nd), but only with sharp research and craft. Fresher angles among recent winners include:
- novel interaction metaphors (async photo booth, embroidery sampler, "a kiss instead of a click")
- civic and government service redesigns
- physical and digital hybrids or emerging-tech concepts (AI social bench, AR flight deck)
- AI-safety-for-users concepts (scam detection)
- under-served health niches (concussion)
- accessibility-first redesigns of real products

### Cited Findings
- **Campus-life and productivity cluster:**
  - Roommate finder (Nest, Rice 2024 2nd). — [Mandy Lai](https://mandyslai.com/rice-designathon)
  - Roommate chore splitting (Rice 2024 Best UI recognition). — [Angela Han](https://medium.com/@angelahan_uiux/a-recap-of-the-rice-design-a-thon-2024-experience-7a4cf82f227f)
  - Habit building (HoneyBuddy, Rice 2025). — [Devpost](https://devpost.com/software/ham)
  - Procrastination and accountability partners (Mango Sticky Rice, Rice 2025). — [Devpost](https://devpost.com/software/rice-design-a-thon-team)
  - Dining-hall app (Eat At State+, MSU 2025). — [Slaughter](https://desireeslaughter.com/academic/reflections-on-msu-designathon-2025/)
  - Food-culture app (Angela Han's 2023 designathon). — [Angela Han 10 lessons](https://medium.com/@angelahan_uiux/10-lessons-learned-from-my-48-hour-designathon-fa5e92fc8086)
- **Sustainability cluster:** surplus food redistribution (FreshEats) and composting (GreenGrid), both UCR 2024 winners. — [UCR blog](https://business.ucr.edu/blog/2024/06/16/ucr-students-organize-first-uiux-design-thon). MSU runs a Sustainability (SDG) track. — [MSU Designathon](https://msu-designathon.devpost.com/)
- **Prompt and track themes set by organizers:**
  - Catalyst 2024 (BU FORGE): Accessibility, Education, Well-Being.
  - Catalyst 2025: Travel, Storytelling, Defining Self-Identity/Individual.
  - UCI 2024: "True to You," including a VR track to build a space your childhood self would love.
  - Sources: [Catalyst 2024 Devpost](https://catalyst-2024.devpost.com/); [Catalyst 2025 Devpost](https://catalyst-2025.devpost.com/); [UCI OIT](https://www.oit.uci.edu/2024/10/11/design-a-thon-story/)
- **Fresh winning angles:**
  - Novel interactions (Common Thread, Duet Booth, TOKYO, "A kiss instead of a click"). — [Figma blog 2026](https://www.figma.com/blog/6-designs-that-reimagine-how-we-interact-with-software/); [Contra](https://contra.com/community/neIDybLs-explore-the-innovative-winners-of-figmas)

  - A physical-digital social bench, AR for pilots, and AI scam detection for users (WUD 2025). — [WUD 2025](https://2026.hci.international/World_Usability_Day.html)

- Generic hackathon advice says judges reward "a fresh angle on a familiar problem" more than a novel problem nobody has, and that winning projects solve "a specific problem for a specific user." *These come from SEO-style blogs and are low reliability.* — [Reskilll](https://blogs.reskilll.com/hackathon-ideas-that-won-2026-judges-actually-pick-examples/)

### Inferences
- **Likely saturated in student design-a-thons:**
  - generic productivity, habit or to-do apps
  - generic mental-health or wellness trackers and chatbots
  - roommate or chore apps
  - campus food or dining apps
  - generic sustainability trackers
  - budgeting apps for students
- These categories recur because the prompts ("empower communities," "well-being," "education") pull every team toward the same student-life problems. An idea in these spaces must name a narrow sub-user and a non-obvious insight to stand out. For example: international students navigating housing leases in a second language, rather than "a roommate app."
- **Fresher directions** consistent with recent winners:
  1. Reimagining an iconic interaction, such as a photo booth, form, checkout or onboarding, with a new metaphor.
  2. Civic or bureaucratic processes that are painful and unglamorous: certificates, benefits, court information, campus administration.
  3. Accessibility-first redesigns of a real, widely used product, with before/after and a WCAG audit.
  4. AI-era safety and trust UX: scam detection, AI disclosure, consent.
  5. Physical-digital or spatial experiences (AR, kiosks, public furniture).
  6. Narrow health or care moments: concussion recovery, caregivers, clinic waiting rooms.
  7. Creative tools and playful craft experiences, which open contests reward under craft categories.
- Whatever the theme, mapping it explicitly to the event's track names and rubric language matters. Devpost and judges both stress tailoring to criteria and requirements.

## 8. Recommended submission structure for a small student team (case study + Figma prototype + demo video + Devpost write-up)

### Takeaway
Package one coherent story across four synced assets:
- a scannable Devpost write-up that mirrors the rubric's headings
- a public, presentation-mode Figma prototype of one polished core flow
- a scripted demo video under 3 minutes
- a compact case study (a Figma page or slides) showing research, rationale, testing and accessibility

Protect the last 4–6 hours for packaging and rehearsal.

### Cited Findings
- Rubric headings to mirror: User Research, Design Thinking, UX, Visual Design (Rice); innovation, aesthetics, service to the challenge, process (Technica). — [Rice 2025](https://rice-design-a-thon-2025.devpost.com/); [Technica 2024](https://technica-2024.devpost.com/)
- Nest (2nd) shipped secondary research, surveys, an interactive prototype and a presentation, plus a design system. It set a hard design cutoff for pitch practice. — [Mandy Lai](https://mandyslai.com/rice-designathon)
- NN/g template: title page, intro, captioned images, results. Show process artefacts and rejected concepts. — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)
- Video under 3 minutes, scripted, with the what and why in the first seconds; upload early. — [Devpost blog](https://info.devpost.com/blog/6-tips-for-making-a-hackathon-demo-video); [Devpost Help](https://help.devpost.com/article/84-video-making-best-practices)
- Figma version history may be checked to confirm the work was made during the event. — [DivHacks](https://divhacks.devpost.com/)
- Devpost advises tailoring the submission to the specific event's criteria. — [Devpost guide](https://info.devpost.com/blog/understanding-hackathon-submission-and-judging-criteria)

### Inferences
- **Devpost write-up skeleton, mirroring Devpost's default "Inspiration / What it does / How we built it / Challenges / Accomplishments / What we learned / What's next" with design-specific content:**
  1. **One-line value proposition and hero image.** Name the target user and the outcome.
  2. **The problem.** One paragraph plus 1–2 statistics or research quotes.
  3. **Research.** Methods with n: survey of about 40, 5 interviews, competitor audit of 3–5 apps. List 3 key insights and 1 persona.
  4. **How might we / design principles.** 3 principles tied to the insights.
  5. **Solution walkthrough.** 3–5 key screens, each with a "because users said X, we did Y" caption.
  6. **Testing and iteration.** 5-user test tasks, success rates, top issues, and before→after screenshots.
  7. **Accessibility.** WCAG 2.2 AA contrast of 4.5:1 checked, touch targets of at least 24 px (ideally larger on mobile), plus any screen-reader or color-blind considerations.
  8. **Design system.** Color, type and components.
  9. **Challenges, what we learned, and what's next.** Include feasibility and an implementation path.
  10. **Links.** Figma prototype (presentation view, public), Figma file (view-only), video, slides.
- **Figma prototype:** build one end-to-end flow, polished and fully clickable. A wide prototype that is half-clickable is worse. Set a starting frame and device frame, and remove dead hotspots. Check access in an incognito window.
- **Case study:** 8–12 slides or a single Figma page following NN/g's order. Include at least one rejected concept to show design thinking.
- **Team split for 3–4 people over 48h:**
  - a research and story lead (also writes the video script and write-up)
  - a UX lead (flows, wireframes, testing)
  - a UI/design-system lead (hi-fi screens, components, accessibility checks)
  - an optional prototyper and video editor
- **Timeline:**
  - research done by about hour 8
  - wireframes by hour 16
  - hi-fi by hour 32
  - tests and iteration by hour 40
  - design freeze, then video, write-up and rehearsal in the final 6–8 hours

### Gaps
- No source directly validates this exact package against judging outcomes. It is synthesized from rubrics, Devpost guidance, NN/g and the Nest case study.
